Loneliness, work pressure and city life

A survey of 180 New Yorkers testing whether longer commutes and multiple jobs go with higher loneliness. They do not. Respondents under more work pressure reported markedly less loneliness, not more.

All four pressures hold up together

Commute time, work hours and job count are related to each other, so a difference in one could be another in disguise. This regression puts them in at once. Each keeps an independent effect, and variance inflation factors are all below 3, so they are not distorting one another.

What would need checking before anyone believed this

  • The effects are implausibly large. A Cohen's d of 2.73 for multiple jobs means the two groups barely overlap. Published loneliness research typically finds correlations between 0.1 and 0.3; several here exceed 0.7. Something other than a genuine population effect may be producing this, and a write-up should say so rather than celebrate it.
  • The scale may be measuring one thing five times. Cronbach's alpha is 0.947 and inter-item correlations run from 0.73 to 0.83. That is usually a sign the items are near-duplicates rather than that the scale is excellent.
  • Direction cannot be established. This is cross-sectional. Working more jobs may reduce loneliness, or less lonely people may take on more work. Nothing here separates the two.
  • Sampling is unknown and Queens is missing. Around 2.3 million residents are unrepresented, and a self-selected form response is not a random sample of New Yorkers.
  • Conditional questions have smaller bases. The perceived impact item has 82 responses, not 180, because it was only shown to app users. Any average from it describes those 82 people.
Source: original Google Form survey, 180 responses, March 2026. Loneliness score is the mean of five UCLA-style items after reverse-coding the two positively worded items. Group comparisons use Welch's t-test; effect sizes are Cohen's d.
